Abstract. Even after harvest, a Farmer Producer Organization (FPO) has to settle
where, when and how much of its members' produce is sold. KisanNiti, a
decision-support framework for this task, is presented. It combines Mandi price
forecasting, storage sensing, and a cooperative allocation model. Mandi prices
from AGMARKNET are treated as a market network and forecast using an
Attention Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (A3T-GCN). Storage
conditions are to be recorded by a low-cost node made of an ESP32 board and an
SHT30 temperature and humidity probe placed in the grain. An integer program
assigns every farmer lot to a mandi, a day and a quantity, and all lots share the
daily limit of each mandi. The forecasting and allocation parts were tested on real
Paddy (Common) prices from 20 Punjab mandis for the 2020–2025 seasons. In
every season, more than 96% of the reported prices were equal to the minimum
support price (MSP). A simple MSP rule, therefore, gave the lowest forecast error
(mean absolute error of Rs. 1.26 per quintal), and no learned model beat it. In a
synthetic 40-lot FPO scenario, the cooperative plan respected every daily limit,
whereas independent choices exceeded a limit in every run. The gain of the plan
over a nearest-mandi rule was small (Rs. 0.79 per quintal) when the limits were
loose and rose to Rs. 3.5–13.9 per quintal when the limits were tight.

1 Introduction
Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) are producer organizations whose members are
farmers. They can pool, market, and sell produce on behalf of the members, giving
small farmers a way to act together in the market [1]. Selling after harvest remains
difficult, and post-harvest losses are large. A 2024 ICRIER policy brief reports that
India loses food worth about Rs. 1.53 trillion every year, based on a national study
carried out by NABCONS during 2020–2022, and identifies storage as one part of the
problem [2].
For an FPO, selling after harvest is not a single decision. Where to sell, when to sell,
and how much to send must be settled together, and the three choices affect one another.
A mandi with a better price may be farther away, and waiting for a later date keeps the

produce in storage for longer. If many farmers send produce in the same few days, one
mandi can receive more than it can handle. Daily wholesale prices are published on the
AGMARKNET portal, and recent Indian work has used these data from 165 markets
for forecasting [3]. For crops bought at the minimum support price (MSP), the
government also announces a fixed price before each season [4][5].
Agricultural price forecasting has improved with modern time-series models.
Statistical, machine learning, and deep learning methods were compared by Manogna
et al. across 23 commodities from 165 Indian markets, and the LSTM and GRU models
performed best [3]. Tomato, onion, and potato prices in India were studied by Nayak et
al., who showed that incorporating temperature and rainfall improved deep learning
models [6]. These studies support data-driven forecasting, but they do not solve the full
selling problem for an FPO.
Another line of research models several price series together. A spectral temporal
graph neural network was applied to daily vegetable prices and trade volumes in Turkey
by Özden and Bulut [7]. Min et al. combined prices of four South Korean crops with
weather variables, and graph-based models outperformed a stacked LSTM [8]. Storage
research has grown in parallel. Temperature and relative humidity inside hermetic
paddy storage were monitored for 125 days by Islam et al. [9], and a recent review
covers the use of AI, IoT and smart sensors in grain storage [10].
Each of these studies solves one part of the problem, and forecasting, storage
monitoring and logistics are mostly treated as separate tasks. In KisanNiti, they are
connected for cooperative selling. Four contributions are made in this paper. First, a
framework is described in which mandi forecasts, farmer lot records, and storage
readings feed into a single selling plan. Second, a mandi network is built from map
coordinates, and an A3T-GCN forecaster is compared with simple baselines on real
AGMARKNET data over three test seasons. Third, the selling decision is written as an
integer program in which farmers share the daily limit of each mandi, and the program
is compared with simple selling rules. Fourth, the circuit of a low-cost storage-sensing
node is presented. The results are reported as they came out, including the finding that
the learned forecasters did not beat simple rules for an MSP crop.
Related work is reviewed in Section 2. The architecture and methods are described
in Section 3. The experiments are reported and discussed in Section 4, and the paper is
concluded in Section 5.

2 Related Work
Agricultural price forecasting has moved from classical time-series models toward
machine learning and deep learning. Daily AGMARKNET wholesale data from 2010
to 2024 for 23 commodities and 165 Indian markets were used by Manogna et al.
ARIMA, support vector regression, XGBoost and several neural networks were
compared, and the lowest errors were obtained with the LSTM and GRU models [3].
Price data for tomato, onion, and potato from major Indian markets were combined with
temperature and precipitation by Nayak et al., and deep-learning models incorporating
these external variables performed better than statistical and machine-learning models
[6].

A different idea introduced by graph-based models is that related series are learned
together rather than one at a time. StemGNN was applied by Özden and Bulut to 1,791
days of Turkish vegetable price and trade volume data and compared with CNN,
LSTM, and Random Forest models [7]. A stacked LSTM was compared by Min et al.
with two graph models, StemGNN and the temporal graph convolutional network (TGCN). In their multivariate setting, the graph models outperformed the LSTM, with the
largest improvement coming from smoothing short-term fluctuations [8]. T-GCN
combines a graph convolution with a gated recurrent unit (GRU) [11], and A3T-GCN
adds an attention step over past time points [12]. Both were first proposed for traffic
forecasting, not for agricultural markets.
Grain-storage research shows that continuous monitoring can support post-harvest
management. A low-cost remote system for hermetic paddy storage was built in
Bangladesh by Islam et al. Over 125 days, the temperature inside the bags stayed
between 16.7 °C and 29.2 °C, and no insect or mould growth was seen [9]. AI and IoT
methods for grain storage, including smart sensors and anomaly detection, were
reviewed by Anukiruthika and Jayas [10].
The value of formal optimisation in agricultural logistics has been shown in several
studies. A mixed-integer linear programming model for harvesting, processing and
transport for small olive growers in Chile was built by Urra-Calfuñir et al. [13]. Firstmile transport for an agricultural cooperative in Greece was organised with a mixedinteger model by Gámez-Albán et al. [14]. A three-objective freight allocation problem
for a food-grain organisation in India, with inventory- and warehouse-related penalty
factors, was formulated by Anoop et al. [15]. For farmer-facing advice, Shams et al.
developed an explainable crop-recommendation system so that the rationale for a
suggested crop can be understood [16].

each paper reports is marked. Market forecasting, storage sensing, and cooperative
allocation are not integrated into a single system in any of the reviewed studies, and
this is the gap addressed here.

3 Proposed System Architecture and Methodology
KisanNiti is built around one question: how can an FPO use market and storage
information to prepare a better sales plan for its members? The overall design is shown
in Fig. 1. Three inputs are used. The first is the mandi price history from
AGMARKNET; the second is the FPO's record of member lots, including the quantity
and location of each lot; and the third is the storage readings from a sensor node. The
price history is fed into a forecasting model, and the lot records are used to calculate
transport costs. Storage readings are meant to feed the plan once the node has been
calibrated. A cooperative allocation model combines the inputs and produces a selling
plan that specifies which lot goes to which mandi, on which day, and in what quantity.

Fig. 1. Overall architecture of KisanNiti: (1) data layer, (2) forecasting and storage risk, (3) fair
allocation, (4) manager approval and WhatsApp messages.

3.1 Market Data and Mandi Network
AGMARKNET data for Paddy (Common) in Punjab for September to December of
2020–2025 were used. The data were downloaded from the official AGMARKNET
API in April 2026 [17]. The raw file had 16,284 rows. After 81 exact duplicate rows
were removed, 19 rows with zero minimum or maximum prices were repaired, and 2
duplicate market-date-variety groups were merged, 16,201 records from 176 mandis
remained. When a mandi reported more than one variety on a day, the arrival-weighted
average of the modal prices was used.
Most mandis report only on some days. The 20 mandis that reported across all six
seasons and had the most trading dates (195 to 288 each) were therefore retained. Each
mandi is one node of a graph. The location of each mandi was obtained from the
OpenStreetMap Nominatim service [18], and each mandi was connected to its k nearest
mandis based on straight-line distance. The value of k was chosen from {2, 3, 5} on a
separate validation season. In this way, the graph has a clear geographic meaning, and
no price relationship is assumed before it is tested. The network is shown in Fig. 2; the
five mandis marked in red are used in the allocation experiment.

Fig. 2. Mandi network used for forecasting: 20 Punjab mandis and the five-nearest-neighbour
graph (red: candidate mandis for the allocation test).

3.2 Graph-Based Price Forecasting
A3T-GCN [12] is used as the main forecaster, and its flow is shown in Fig. 3. It has
three parts. Each mandi's values are mixed with those of its neighbours by a graph
convolution. How these values change over time is learned by a GRU. A weight is then
given to each past time step by an attention layer before the forecast is made. Let A be

The input is the last 7 trading-date prices of all 20 mandis, and the output is the price
of every mandi for the next 7 trading dates. Because the MSP rises every season, each
input window is divided by its latest price, and the relative change from that price is
predicted. The scale is thereby kept the same across seasons. The model is trained to
reduce the mean absolute error, and only prices that were actually reported are used as
targets. Gaps in the inputs are filled with the last reported price from the same mandi
for that season. Before the first report of a mandi in a season, the mean price reported
by the other mandis on that date is used, so that no future value enters the input.
3.3 Storage-Sensing Node
Storage conditions matter when waiting for a later selling date is considered. Regularly
checking probes in stored grain is advised in grain storage guidance because hot spots
may indicate insects [19]. A low-cost node was therefore designed, as shown in Fig. 4.
An ESP32 development board with built-in 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi (802.11 b/g/n) is used [20].
A protected SHT30 probe is pushed into the grain, and temperature and relative
humidity are read over the I²C bus (GPIO 21 and 22). A typical tolerance of ±2% RH
between 20 and 80% RH and ±0.2 °C between 0 and 65 °C is stated for this sensor [21].
The relative humidity of the air between the kernels is in equilibrium with the grain,
and divided by 100 it gives the water activity of the grain. Storage fungi need a relative
humidity of at least 65% [22], and grain kept in air below 65–70% relative humidity
degrades slowly [23]. A reading is taken every 5 minutes, and a simple rule maps the
24-hour median to a low, medium or high risk level at 65% and 70% RH.

Fig. 4. Storage-sensing node: SHT30 probe in the grain, ESP32 board, Wi-Fi link to an MQTT
broker with TLS, and the dashboard and WhatsApp alert for the manager.

The same firmware was run in the Wokwi ESP32 simulator [24], so that the node
can be reproduced without hardware. In this version, the node has not yet been placed
in real storage, and its readings are not used in the experiments of Section 4.
Temperature and humidity do not measure grain moisture or spoilage directly, so a
storage-condition term is to be added to the allocation model only after the node has
been calibrated against measured grain samples.

3.4 Cooperative Selling Allocation Model
The selling decision is written as an integer program. Let f be a farmer lot, m a candidate
mandi and d = 0, 1, …, D − 1 a trading day in the planning period. Qf is the quantity of
lot f, p̂md the forecast price at mandi m on day d, κfm the road distance from lot f to mandi
m, r the transport cost per quintal per kilometre, and w a small waiting cost per quintal
per day. The net value of sending one quintal is

By constraint (7), every lot in the planning run is sold exactly once. Constraint (8)
makes the model cooperative: Cmd is the quantity the FPO can deliver to mandi m on
day d, and this limit is shared by all lots. Without (8), each lot would go to its own best
mandi and day, and the problem would split into separate farmer decisions. Quantities
are planned in 10-quintal units, which is close to how loads are sent. The model is
solved with the HiGHS solver [25] through SciPy [26]. Storage and market-arrival
terms are not included yet. They are to be added once the storage node is calibrated and
the effect of arrivals on price has been measured for the selected mandis.
3.5 Recommendation Output
The output of the model is a table with one row for each part of a lot: the lot, the mandi,
the selling day and the quantity, together with the forecast price and the transport cost
used. This structured output can later be shown on an FPO dashboard or sent to farmers
as messages. If explanations are added, they should say why a plan was chosen, in the
spirit of explainable recommendation systems [16].

Forward-chaining evaluation was used. Each test season was forecast using models
trained on all earlier seasons except the one immediately preceding it, which was
reserved for validation. Three test seasons: 2023 (trained on 2020–2021, validated on
2022), 2024, and 2025. Seasons were never mixed in a single input window. Seven
models were compared: (i) a naive forecast that repeats the last price; (ii) the mean of
the last 7 prices; (iii) an MSP rule that always predicts the Grade A MSP of the season,
which is announced before the season; (iv) ridge regression on the relative changes,
with its penalty chosen on validation; (v) a GRU without a graph; (vi) T-GCN, which
is the graph model without attention; and (vii) A3T-GCN. The neural models had a
hidden size of 32 and were trained with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001, batch
size 32) for up to 400 epochs, and training was stopped when the validation error had
not improved for 40 epochs. Each neural model was trained with five random seeds.
The mean absolute error (MAE), the root mean squared error (RMSE) and the mean
absolute percentage error (MAPE) are reported over forecast horizons of 1 to 7 trading
dates, scored only on prices that were actually reported. The experiments were run with
Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.9.1 and SciPy 1.15.2.
4.2 Forecasting Results
Table 3 gives the results. Validation selected k = 5 for the 2023 season and k = 3 for
2024 and 2025. All errors are small, below Rs. 7 per quintal on average, because the
prices hardly move. The lowest MAE in all three seasons came from the MSP rule
(mean Rs. 1.26 per quintal), which also had the lowest RMSE. The naive forecast was
second (Rs. 1.77). Among the learned models, the most accurate was A3T-GCN (Rs.
2.36), followed by the GRU (Rs. 2.51), T-GCN (Rs. 2.54) and ridge regression (Rs.
4.09). The graph alone (T-GCN) gave nothing over a plain GRU, and the small edge of

A date-by-date comparison at a horizon of one trading date was also made. On each
of the 158 test dates, the error of A3T-GCN was higher than that of the naive forecast.
The naive forecast is exactly right whenever a mandi stays at the MSP, which is almost
always, while a change of exactly zero is rarely predicted by a neural model. The MSP
rule tied with the naive forecast on 126 dates was better on 13 and worse on 19, but its
misses were smaller, which gave it the lower average error. For an MSP crop such as
Paddy (Common), the price is therefore almost fixed by policy, and the announced MSP
was the best forecast found.
4.3 Cooperative Allocation Results
Real member records were not available, so a synthetic FPO was built to test the
allocation model. It has 40 lots placed at random in an area of about 30 km × 30 km
between Patiala and Bhawanigarh, with lot sizes from 20 to 120 quintals (2,815 quintals
per FPO on average). The candidate mandis are the five marked in red in Fig. 2: Patiala
(New Anaj Mandi), Patran, Bhawanigarh, Chanarthal and Amargarh. The settings
below are assumptions for this test and not measured values. Road distance is taken as
1.3 times the straight-line distance, transport costs Rs. 2 per quintal per kilometre,
waiting costs Rs. 2 per quintal per day, and at most 300 quintals can be delivered by
the FPO to each mandi per day. Each plan covers the next 5 trading dates. Plans were
made from 16 starting dates in the 2025 season for 10 random FPOs (160 runs per
policy), and every plan was scored with the actual 2025 prices. When a mandi did not
report on a date, its last reported price was used.
Four policies were compared. In P1, each lot is sent to its nearest mandi on the first
day that still has room, and lots are served in random order. In P2, every lot chooses its
own best mandi and day without looking at the others. P3 is the KisanNiti model of
Sect. 3.4, run once with the MSP rule as the price forecast and once with the A3T-GCN
forecast. P4 is the same model given the actual prices, which is an upper bound that no
real system can reach. A separate check confirmed that every plan sold each lot exactly
once, and any day on which the limit of a mandi was exceeded was counted. Each run
of the model, with 1,000 integer variables, was solved in about 0.25 s on a laptop.

Table 4 shows three things. Independent choices (P2) do not suit a cooperative:
when every lot goes to its own best option, almost all produce reaches the nearest
mandis on the first day, and the daily limits were exceeded in all 160 runs (on two
mandi-days per run on average). The cooperative model (P3) respected every limit and
earned Rs. 0.79 per quintal more than the nearest-mandi rule, mostly through less travel
and waiting. That gain is small and varies between runs (standard deviation Rs. 1.05).
The forecast, finally, hardly changed the plan: P3 with the MSP rule, P3 with A3TGCN and the perfect-foresight bound were within Rs. 0.01 per quintal of each other,
because the prices at the five mandis were nearly the same.

The settings above are assumptions, so the test was repeated with transport costs of
Rs. 1, 2 and 4 per quintal per kilometre and daily limits of 200, 300 and 500 quintals
(Fig. 5). The gain depends mainly on how tight the daily limit is. With a limit of 500
quintals, the nearest-mandi rule was already almost optimal (gain of Rs. 0.15 per quintal
or less). With a limit of 200 quintals, the gain was Rs. 3.49, 6.94 and 13.91 per quintal
at the three transport costs, and the average distance fell from 19.9 km to about 16.5
km.

4.4 Discussion and Limitations
The first version teaches one clear lesson. Mandi prices of an MSP crop such as Paddy
(Common) in Punjab are almost fixed by policy, so a complex forecaster adds nothing
and the announced MSP can serve as the price. What remains useful for such crops is
coordination: deliveries are spread over mandis and days, limits are kept, and travel is
reduced. The forecasting branch is more likely to matter for crops with market-set
prices, such as basmati paddy or wheat held on the farm after harvest, and testing it on
those crops is the next step.
The study has clear limits. Only one crop, one state and the September to December
period of six seasons are covered by the price data. The modal price published for a
mandi is not the exact price that a given farmer receives. The FPO, its lots, the transport
rate, the road factor, the waiting cost and the daily limits are synthetic, so the allocation
gains show what the model can do under these settings and not what a real FPO will
earn. The storage node has been tested only in simulation. Finally, the way in which
traders or mandis would react if many farmers followed the plan cannot be shown by a
historical test.

5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
KisanNiti has been presented as a framework that helps an FPO decide where, when
and how much of its members' produce is to be sold. Three parts are joined in it: a
mandi-network price forecaster based on A3T-GCN, a low-cost storage-sensing node
built around an ESP32 and an SHT30 probe, and an integer program that assigns farmer
lots to mandis and days while the daily limit of each mandi is shared. The forecasting
and allocation parts were tested on real AGMARKNET prices for Paddy (Common)
from 20 Punjab mandis over 2020–2025.
Two results were obtained. The price of this crop is set almost entirely by the MSP:
in every season, more than 96% of the reported prices were equal to it, and the
announced MSP forecast better than every learned model, A3T-GCN included. A
forecasting system for FPOs should check whether a crop is sold at the MSP before any
learned model is used, and the announced MSP should be taken as the price if it is. The
cooperative allocation model, in turn, respected the shared daily limits every time,
while independent choices broke them in every run. Its gain over the nearest-mandi rule
was small with loose limits (Rs. 0.79 per quintal) and grew to Rs. 3.5–13.9 per quintal
with tight ones. For MSP crops, KisanNiti therefore helps through coordination, not
through price prediction.
Several steps remain. The most important is to test the forecaster on crops whose
prices are set by the market, such as basmati paddy and wheat held after harvest, where
timing and mandi choice can matter more; a longer and wider AGMARKNET extract
than the one available for this study is needed for it. Next, the storage node is to be
assembled and calibrated against measured grain samples, so that a storage-condition
term can be added to the allocation model in a tested way. The effect of market arrivals
on price should also be included once it has been measured for the selected mandis. The
synthetic FPO should be replaced with real member records, transport quotes and mandi
handling limits from a partner FPO, and the plan should be checked against what
farmers actually earn. Finally, the selling plan is to be delivered to farmers as short

messages in their own language, with a simple explanation of each recommendation
[16], and its usefulness is to be studied with FPO staff and members.
